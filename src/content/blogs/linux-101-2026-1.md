I think a lot of us walked into Linux 101 expecting to learn a few terminal commands, install Ubuntu or Virtual Box, and maybe finally understand what people meant when they casually mentioned Linux in conversations about cloud computing or cybersecurity.

Five days later, I don't think any of us left talking only about commands.

Organized by the IEEE Computer Society Pulchowk Student Branch Chapter, Linux 101 was a five day hands-on workshop that introduced us to Linux fundamentals, networking, system administration, troubleshooting, automation, and containerization. But somewhere between navi\\gating directories, fixing broken commands, and asking "why isn't this working?" for the tenth time, the workshop became much more than learning an operating system.

It became a reminder that the best way to learn technology is simply to start.

## **Every Expert Started Somewhere**

There is something oddly intimidating about opening a terminal for the first time. It is just a blinking cursor, yet it somehow feels like one wrong command could destroy everything.

That feeling slowly disappeared over the course of the workshop.

Every session built naturally on the previous one. We started by understanding the Linux filesystem and learning the commands that seemed confusing only a few hours earlier. Soon we were managing packages, creating users, monitoring processes, writing simple Bash scripts, troubleshooting networks, and finally exploring automation and system services.

None of these topics felt rushed because every concept came with an opportunity to try it ourselves.

Sometimes it worked the first time. Most of the time it didn't. Oddly enough, those moments taught us the most.

## **Learning Happens Best When People Are Willing to Help**

One thing that stood out throughout the workshop was the environment the mentors created.

No question felt too basic.

No mistake felt embarrassing.

Whether someone forgot a command, accidentally broke a configuration, or simply wanted to know why Linux behaved the way it did, there was always someone willing to explain it with patience.

The sessions never felt like lectures where we copied commands from a screen. They felt like conversations.

And I think that made all the difference.

## **Behind Every Workshop Is Someone Who Believed In It**

Events like Linux 101 are easy to appreciate as participants because we only see what happens inside the classroom.

What we often don't see are the people and organizations who make those opportunities possible in the first place.

We would like to sincerely thank TekBay Digital for supporting Linux 101 and believing in student driven learning initiatives like this one.

Their sponsorship went far beyond helping organize an event. It allowed our mentors, who dedicated their time throughout five days of sessions, to be appreciated with mentor tokens for the effort they invested into every participant's learning. They also sponsored prizes for quiz winners and for participants whose consistency and dedication stood out through their note taking and engagement during the workshop.

While these may seem like small gestures, they meant a lot.

Being recognized for staying curious, asking questions, taking notes, or performing well in a quiz makes learning feel rewarding. More importantly, it creates an environment where students are encouraged to participate rather than simply attend.

Industry support is often talked about in terms of sponsorships and funding, but its real impact is much simpler than that.

It creates opportunities that students remember long after the workshop ends.

We are grateful to TekBay Digital for choosing to invest in students and for helping make Linux 101 a learning experience that felt both meaningful and memorable.

## **Looking Back**

Linux 101 taught us how to navigate directories, manage users, troubleshoot networks, and automate tasks. But I think what many of us will remember most is something much simpler. The confidence to open a terminal without feeling intimidated. The confidence to try something new even when we weren't sure it would work.

Because every person working in cloud computing, cybersecurity, DevOps, or software engineering once looked at that same blinking cursor without knowing what to type next.

For many of us, Linux 101 was where we typed that first command.
